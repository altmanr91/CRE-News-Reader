The New York Housing Court Problem Comes Home to City Hall

Commercial Observer — Mon, 19 Oct 2026 13:29:05 +0000

SUMMARY

Opinion / Market Commentary

This article argues that the inefficiencies of New York City's Housing Court system are a significant impediment to housing development and economic productivity, a problem now acutely felt by the city government itself in its efforts to redevelop public housing. The author contends that lengthy resolution times for cases, averaging 15 months, impose substantial costs on property owners through lost rent and ongoing expenses, effectively diminishing the economic value of real estate. This issue is particularly ironic as the city simultaneously advocates for increased housing production while its legal system hinders the productive use of existing units. The article suggests that the city's official vacancy rate of 1.4% is misleading because it doesn't account for units tied up in litigation or occupied by non-paying tenants, which are physically occupied but economically impaired. The author concludes that a more robust and efficient Housing Court, with increased judicial capacity and resources, is essential for both private owners and the city's broader affordable housing goals.

A Federal Banking Rule Threatens New York Affordable Housing Investment

Commercial Observer — Mon, 19 Oct 2026 13:12:03 +0000

SUMMARY

Opinion / Market Commentary

A proposed federal banking rule threatens to reduce private investment in affordable housing projects in New York by weakening incentives for regional and community banks to utilize the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC). The rule, proposed by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), would alter the Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) for banks with assets between $1.65 billion and $10 billion. This change could significantly impact the financing of affordable housing, as LIHTC is the largest source of such funding nationwide, with banks providing 75-80% of its equity, largely motivated by CRA considerations. The article argues that this regulatory shift would exacerbate New York's housing insecurity crisis, particularly in smaller cities and rural areas where regional banks are crucial investors.
